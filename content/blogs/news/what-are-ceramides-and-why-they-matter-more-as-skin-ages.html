What Are Ceramides and Why They Matter More as Skin Ages

Ceramides are essential to healthy, youthful-looking skin, and their levels naturally decline with age. Learn what they do, why they matter, and how to support your skin.

Most people have heard of ceramides in moisturizers, but fewer understand what they actually are or why the skin can start to run lower on them with age. Just as you might know about antioxidants and collagen, ceramides are a foundational skin health topic that’s getting a refreshed spotlight as many people start to shift their focus toward beauty from within.

What Are Ceramides?

Ceramides are lipid (fat) molecules that occur naturally in your skin and make up a large portion of its outermost layer, which is called the stratum corneum. You can think of ceramides as the "mortar" between your skin cells, as they hold everything together and form the lipid barrier that keeps moisture in and potential environmental irritants out.

Without enough ceramides, the skin barrier becomes compromised and more vulnerable. Skin loses hydration, feels rougher, and becomes more reactive to the elements. Ceramides differ from other skin-health buzzwords like collagen (which provides structural support) or hyaluronic acid (which draws water into the skin), as they’re specifically about barrier integrity and retaining moisture.

​

Why Ceramide Levels Decline with Age

It’s no secret that the skin of your twenties doesn’t maintain its moisture and elasticity quite the same way in your forties, fifties, and beyond. This also means skin doesn't maintain its ceramide supply indefinitely. Natural ceramide levels begin to decline with age, and the factors compound over time.

Several factors drive changes to the skin barrier:

  • Biological aging: The skin gradually produces fewer lipids, including ceramides, as part of the natural aging process.
  • UV exposure: Sun damage degrades the skin's lipid barrier over time, especially if appropriate UV protection hasn’t been used.
  • Environmental stressors: Pollution and harsh weather negatively influence of the skin barrier.
  • Lifestyle factors:  Poor sleep, a diet low in essential fatty acids, smoking, and habits like over-cleansing or taking long hot showers can all chip away at the skin's natural lipid barrier over time.

The result shows up as the most common complaints of aging skin, including increased dryness, loss of elasticity, rougher texture, more visible fine lines, and a barrier that's slower to bounce back.

Topical vs. Ingestible Ceramides

For years, ceramides have been a staple of dermatologist-recommended moisturizers and serums, where they work by replenishing the skin's surface barrier from the outside in. Research supports their efficacy in topical form, particularly for improving hydration and supporting a more resilient skin barrier.

More recently, oral (ingestible) ceramide supplements have emerged as a complementary approach. When ceramides are taken by mouth, they're broken down during digestion, and the resulting components are absorbed through the bloodstream, where they're believed to support the skin's own lipid production — a different pathway than topical products entirely.

The two approaches don't compete; they target the same goal from different directions, which is why pairing a topical routine with an ingestible supplement is increasingly how skin health professionals think about barrier support.
